Unsupervised learning

Every model so far has learned from labeled data, where each row comes with the answer, like lemon or orange. This is called supervised learning.

In unsupervised learning there are no labels, so the model has to find patterns in the features on its own.

Each dot is a person at a music festival, placed where their phone was at 8 pm. Nobody told us who is with which crowd. Press Find groups and an algorithm will look for 4 groups on its own.

k-means

Where to park the food trucks

Say you have 3 food trucks at the festival, and each person walks to whichever truck is closest. Drag the trucks around to make the average walk as short as you can.

The best spots end up in the middle of the crowds, and k-means is a way to find them. Each truck is a cluster center, and everyone who walks to the same truck is in the same cluster.

k-means

How k-means works

  1. Put k centers in random spots, where k is the number of clusters you want
  2. Give each point to its closest center
  3. Move each center to the middle of its points, which is the average of their positions

Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the centers stop moving. Each round, the centers get pulled a little closer to the middle of a crowd.

k-means

A bad start

Because the centers start in random spots, some runs get stuck. Two centers can end up sharing one crowd while another center has to cover two crowds.

To tell a good run from a bad one, add up the squared distance from each point to its center. This total is called the inertia, and a lower inertia means the points are closer to their centers.

scikit-learn runs k-means several times from different starts and keeps the run with the lowest inertia. n_init sets how many times it runs.

k-means

How many clusters?

You have to pick k yourself, but adding a cluster always lowers the inertia, because every point gets a center that's at least as close. If you picked the k with the lowest inertia, you'd end up with one cluster per point.

Instead, plot the inertia for each k and look for the elbow, where the line stops dropping quickly. Past the elbow, extra clusters only split up groups that belong together.

k-means

The silhouette score

The silhouette score is another way to choose k. It checks how well each point fits in its own cluster compared to the next closest cluster, using two numbers:

  • a, the average distance to the other points in its cluster
  • b, the average distance to the points in the closest other cluster
silhouette = (b − a) / the bigger of a and b

A point well inside its cluster has a small a and a big b, so its silhouette is close to 1. A point on the border between two clusters has an a and b that are about the same, so its silhouette is close to 0.

The silhouette score is the average over all the points, and the k with the highest score is a good choice. Click a person to see their numbers.

k-means in scikit-learn

This clusters the fruits in fruits.csv without using the fruit column. There's nothing to predict, so there's no y and no train_test_split.

k-means uses distances, so the features are scaled first, like with KNN.

k-means numbers the clusters but doesn't tell you what they mean. To find out, look at the average of each feature in each cluster. Here cluster 1 has the taller, lighter fruits.

Since we do have the fruit column, pd.crosstab can check the clusters against it, and cluster 1 turns out to be mostly lemons.

k-means

Where k-means struggles

Later that night, there's a crowd at the stage, a line of people walking along the curved path to the parking lot, and a group at the gate. A few people are wandering around on their own.

Every point goes to its closest center, so the border between two clusters is always a straight line. This works well for round groups of about the same size, but not for a long, curved path.

k-means also has to put every point in a cluster, so the wanderers get added to whichever cluster is closest.

Hierarchical clustering

Merging step by step

Hierarchical clustering builds clusters by merging. Every point starts as its own cluster, and each step merges the two clusters that are closest together, until everything is in one cluster.

The tree under the plot is called a dendrogram, and it records every merge. The height where two branches join is how far apart those clusters were when they merged, so close points join near the bottom and groups that are far apart join near the top.

Hierarchical clustering

Cutting the tree

The dendrogram holds every possible clustering, from one cluster per point to one big cluster. To pick one, cut across it with a line, and each branch the line crosses becomes a cluster.

A good place to cut is a tall gap, where the line can move a long way without crossing a join. A tall gap means those groups stayed apart for a long time before they merged.

In scikit-learn, AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=4) does the merging and the cut, and scipy can draw the dendrogram:

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram
dendrogram(linkage(X, "ward"))
plt.show()

Hierarchical clustering

Linkage

To find the two closest clusters, we need a way to measure the distance between two groups of points. This choice is called the linkage.

Single linkage uses the distance between the closest pair of points, one from each cluster. A cluster can grow one nearby point at a time, so it can follow long shapes like these queues. At night, though, the wanderers form a chain between the groups, and almost everyone ends up in one cluster.

Ward linkage merges the two clusters that would add the least to the inertia. It makes round clusters like k-means does, and it's the default in scikit-learn.

Density

DBSCAN

Density-based clustering says a cluster is a crowded area with emptier space around it. The crowd can be any shape, as long as the points are packed close together.

DBSCAN checks how crowded the spot around each point is. It draws a circle of radius eps around the point, and if at least min_samples points are inside, the point is in a crowded spot.

Crowded points within eps of each other join the same cluster, so a cluster keeps growing as long as the crowd keeps going. This is how it follows the curved path.

Points that aren't close to any crowd are called noise, and they're left out of every cluster. You don't choose the number of clusters, since it depends on how big eps is. Click a point to see its circle.

Density

HDBSCAN

DBSCAN uses the same eps everywhere, so it expects every cluster to be about as crowded as the others. Here two tight crowds at the food stands are next to a looser group sitting on the lawn.

With a small eps, the tight crowds are found but most of the lawn is called noise. With an eps big enough for the lawn, the circles around the two tight crowds overlap and they merge into one.

HDBSCAN tries every eps, from small to large, and watches how the clusters merge as eps grows. It keeps the clusters that last the longest before merging, so it can find a tight crowd and a loose group at the same time. Instead of eps, you set min_cluster_size, the smallest group you'd still call a cluster.

Gaussian mixtures

Stretched clusters

People at the food stands are waiting in three long lines. k-means looks for round clusters, so it cuts across the lines instead of along them.

A Gaussian mixture assumes each cluster is a cloud of points shaped like a bell curve, thick in the middle and thinning out toward the edges. Each cloud can stretch and tilt to fit its points, like the ellipses here, so it can fit long, thin lines.

Gaussian mixtures

Soft clustering

k-means puts every point in exactly one cluster, even a point sitting right on the border.

A Gaussian mixture knows how far each point is from the middle of each cloud, so it can give it a chance of being in each one, like 70% and 30%. This is called soft clustering, and it shows how sure the model is.

Here it splits the fruits into 2 clusters. The faded fruits are near the border, where the model isn't sure. Click a fruit to see its chances.

Clustering

No method wins everywhere

Each plot has a score for how well it matches the real groups, from 0 for random to 1 for a perfect match. Hollow points are noise.

On round crowds that are far apart, every method does well. On the other shapes the scores spread out, and a different method comes out on top each time.

With your own data there are no real groups to check against. Plot what you can, think about what shape the groups might have, and try more than one method.

Clustering

Pick a method

  • k-means for round groups, and it's fast on big data
  • Hierarchical clustering to see groups inside bigger groups, but it's slow on big data
  • DBSCAN for groups of any shape, with noise left out
  • Gaussian mixtures for stretched or overlapping groups, with a chance for each point

Pick a method for each of these.

Dimensionality reduction

Too many features

Lots of features make models slower and more likely to overfit, and there's no way to plot them all at once.

Distances also stop working well. This chart shows how far one random point is from 199 others. As you add features, the distances bunch together until every point is about the same distance away. Then "closest" doesn't mean much, which hurts methods like KNN and k-means. This is called the curse of dimensionality.

Dimensionality reduction squashes lots of features into a few, while keeping as much of the information as it can.

Dimensionality reduction

Squashing two features into one

These are the height and arm span of 50 people. Taller people usually have longer arms, so the two go up together.

To describe each person with one number, we can draw a line through the middle and slide each person onto it. A line along the cloud keeps the people spread out, so you can still tell them apart. A line across it squashes them together.

The line that keeps the most spread is called the first principal component. PCA, short for principal component analysis, finds it for you, and then finds the next best line at a right angle to it.

Dimensionality reduction

How many components?

These are images of handwritten digits. Each one is 8 by 8 pixels, so it has 64 features. PCA turns them into 64 components, sorted from the one that keeps the most spread to the one that keeps the least.

The bars show how much each component keeps, and the line adds them up. A common rule is to keep enough components for 90% of the spread, which is 21 here.

The bottom row rebuilds the digits from only the components you keep. The first few give the rough shape, and the later ones add the details.

Dimensionality reduction

Digits in 2D

With two components, each 64-pixel image becomes a single point you can plot. Each number here is one image, colored by the digit it shows.

PCA never saw the labels, but most of the 0s, 4s and 6s still end up near each other. Other digits overlap, since two components only keep 29% of the spread.

t-SNE is another method made for plots like this, and its groups are much cleaner. It's only for looking, though. It can't place new data on the same plot, so it can't go before a model like PCA can.

Dimensionality reduction

PCA in scikit-learn

PCA works like StandardScaler, with fit and transform. n_components can be the number of components to keep, or a share of the spread like 0.9.

PCA looks for spread, so scale your features first if they're in different units. Otherwise the feature with the biggest numbers takes over.

PCA can also go in a pipeline before a model. Here KNN scores about the same with 10 components as with all 64 pixels.
