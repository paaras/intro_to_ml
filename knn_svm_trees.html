Three ways to draw a boundary

Last lesson, logistic regression split the data with a straight line. The models in this lesson can draw curved or blocky boundaries instead.

We'll use fruits.csv, which has the width and height of 60 lemons and oranges. Lemons are usually taller and thinner.

The shading shows what a model would predict for a new fruit at that spot

k-nearest neighborsLooks at the closest fruits
Support vector machineLeaves as much room as possible between the groups
Decision treeAsks a series of yes/no questions

k-nearest neighbors

Ask the neighbors

To classify a new fruit, look at the k closest fruits and go with the majority

This is k-nearest neighbors, or KNN. With k = 5, if 3 of the 5 closest fruits are lemons, the new fruit is a lemon.

Training a KNN model just stores the data, and the distances only get worked out when you make a prediction. You can click the plot to try a new fruit.

k-nearest neighbors

How many neighbors?

k is the number of neighbors that get a vote. With k = 1 the closest fruit decides, so an orange sitting among the lemons gets a small orange patch around it. This is overfitting, where the model learns quirks of the training data instead of the general pattern.

A bigger k smooths the boundary out, but if k gets too big, faraway fruits start to outvote the nearby ones. The best k is usually somewhere in the middle.

k-nearest neighbors

Features need to be on the same scale

Here we use weight in grams and height in cm. A 20 g difference is small for a fruit, but to KNN it looks much bigger than a 1 cm difference in height, so weight ends up deciding almost everything.

To fix this, scale the features first. StandardScaler puts them on the same scale so they count about equally.

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

model = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),
    KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5),
)

A pipeline runs the scaler and then the model, and you use it like any other model

Support vector machines

Which line is best?

An SVM starts with a straight line. In this small example lots of lines separate the lemons from the oranges, and A, B and C all get all 16 fruits right.

But a line that runs right next to a group will get new fruit near that group wrong, so it's safer to stay as far from both groups as possible.

Support vector machines

The widest street

An SVM picks the line with the widest empty street around it

Think of the line as the middle of a street. The street gets as wide as it can before it hits a fruit, and its width is called the margin.

Only the fruits on the edges of the street matter. They're called support vectors, and moving any other fruit a little won't change the line.

Support vector machines

When no straight line works

Sometimes no straight line will do. Here the dots are people, placed by how far the thermostat was from 21°C. People near 0 are comfortable, and people far away in either direction aren't.

With a single feature, a boundary is just a cut, and no cut can put the comfortable people on their own side.

Now add a second feature, x². Points far from 0 shoot up and the ones near 0 stay low, so a straight line can split them. That line crosses the curve twice, which gives you two cuts back on the original line.

Support vector machines

Adding a third feature

Same idea with two features. wifi.csv has the position of 90 phones and whether they connected. The connected ones are in a circle around the router, so no straight line can separate them.

Add a third feature, z = x² + y², the squared distance from the router. Faraway phones rise, nearby ones stay low, and a flat sheet fits in between.

A flat cut is a line in 2D and a plane in 3D, and with more dimensions it's called a hyperplane. An SVM always makes a flat cut like this, sometimes in a space with added features.

From straight above, the sheet meets the bowl in a circle, and that circle is the boundary in 2D

Support vector machines

The kernel trick

Adding features by hand only works if you can guess the right feature, and usually you can't.

An SVM doesn't actually need the coordinates of the points, only how similar pairs of points are. A kernel calculates that similarity as if the points had been lifted into a bigger space, without actually doing the lifting. The SVM finds a flat cut in that bigger space, which shows up as a curved boundary in the original one.

Support vector machines

SVM in scikit-learn

The SVM classifier is called SVC. You choose the kernel with kernel, the default is "rbf", and degree sets the power for the polynomial kernel.

model.score predicts on the test data and gives you the accuracy

Decision trees

A decision tree asks questions

A decision tree works like a game of 20 questions

It asks a yes/no question about a feature, then another question depending on the answer, until it reaches a leaf. The leaf predicts whichever fruit was most common there in the training data.

The questions look like "is the width at most 6.85?", so they cut the plot straight across or straight down. That's why tree boundaries are made of boxes.

You can click the plot to follow a fruit down the tree

Decision trees

How the tree picks a question

The tree tries all the features and cut points and picks the cut that separates lemons from oranges best. Ideally the lemons would all end up on one side and the oranges on the other.

A simple way to score a cut is to have both sides guess their most common fruit and count the mistakes. The tree picks the cut with the fewest, then repeats the process on both sides.

scikit-learn uses a slightly different score called Gini impurity, but the idea is the same

Decision trees

Deep trees overfit

If you let a tree keep going, it will keep asking questions until all the boxes are pure. By then it's drawing tiny boxes around single fruits, which is memorizing rather than learning.

max_depth limits how many questions in a row the tree can ask. Here it trains on the filled fruits and is tested on the hollow ones.

As the depth goes up, training accuracy goes all the way to 100%, but test accuracy goes up and then back down. That's overfitting again, just like k = 1.

Cross-validation

A test score can be lucky

Last lesson we held back 25% of the rows as test data. Which rows end up in the test set is random, and with a small dataset that matters a lot.

This is the same KNN model (k = 5) scored on different random splits, with the circled fruits as the test set

The score jumps around even though nothing about the model changed. So if a model beats another by a few points on a single split, it might just have gotten an easier test.

Cross-validation

How cross-validation works

Cross-validation gets around this by testing on all the data, not just a random chunk.

You split the data into 5 parts called folds. Train on 4 and test on the 5th, then rotate until all 5 folds have been the test set.

Here it's a decision tree with max_depth=3. You get 5 scores, and their average is a much more reliable estimate than a single split. The spread tells you how much the score depends on luck.

A fold holds 12 fruits, blue is the test fold and gray is training

Cross-validation

Using cross-validation to choose k

Now we can pick k properly. The black line is accuracy on the training data, which is 100% at k = 1 since a fruit's nearest neighbor is itself, so it's no help for picking k.

The blue line is the cross-validation score, which only tests on fruits the model didn't train on. It's lowest at k = 1, best around k = 7 to 9, and fairly flat after that.

Pick the k with the best cross-validation score. The same approach works for max_depth, the SVM kernel, or choosing between models.

Cross-validation

Cross-validation in scikit-learn

cross_val_score does the whole loop for you. You give it an unfitted model and all of X and y, so there's no train_test_split.

It returns a score per fold, and you compare settings using the mean. Once you've picked the best k, fit the final model on all the data.

ROC and AUC

The ROC curve

Last lesson, moving the threshold traded off two kinds of mistakes. The ROC curve shows all the thresholds at once.

Here a spam filter has scored 40 emails, 20 spam and 20 real. Anything at or above the threshold goes to the spam folder.

For any threshold we get two numbers. The true positive rate is the share of spam caught (recall), and the false positive rate is the share of real email wrongly flagged. Bringing the threshold down from 1 to 0 traces out the ROC curve.

ROC and AUC

AUC: the area under the curve

A good model scores spam high and real email low, so as the threshold comes down it catches most of the spam before it flags much real email. Its curve goes up toward the top left corner.

A model that can't tell them apart follows the diagonal, which is no better than a coin flip. AUC is the area under the curve, so 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 is guessing. Unlike accuracy, it doesn't depend on picking a threshold.

ROC and AUC

What AUC means

AUC is the chance that a random spam email gets a higher score than a random real email

So AUC is really about ranking. If spam usually scores above real email, there's some threshold that will work well.

If you draw random pairs and count how often the spam wins, you get closer and closer to the AUC. Checking all 400 pairs gives it exactly.

ROC and AUC

ROC and AUC in scikit-learn

ROC needs scores, not yes/no predictions. predict_proba(X)[:, 1] gives the probability of the positive class, which is lemon here.

roc_curve gives you the points you traced by hand, and scoring="roc_auc" makes cross_val_score use AUC instead of accuracy
